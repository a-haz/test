Why your attachment is too big

A 12-megapixel photo is roughly 4,000 × 3,000 pixels. That's far more detail than anyone needs to view it on a screen or print at normal sizes — but it's why each file is several megabytes. Two levers shrink it dramatically:

  • Resize (fewer pixels). Halving the width quarters the pixel count, which alone usually cuts file size 60–75%.
  • Re-compress (more efficient encoding). Saving as JPEG at 80% quality discards detail your eye won't miss.

For most emails, 1,920 px wide at 80% JPEG quality is the sweet spot: it looks crisp full-screen yet is roughly 10× smaller than the original.

Which format should you use?

  • JPEG — the safe default for photos. Universally supported, small files.
  • WebP — 25–35% smaller than JPEG at the same quality, supported by all modern email clients and browsers. Great if you want the smallest possible attachment.
  • PNG — best for screenshots, logos, and graphics with sharp edges or transparency. Avoid it for photos; files get large.

FAQ

Will compressing reduce the quality visibly?

At 80% JPEG quality and a sensible width, no — the difference is invisible at normal viewing sizes while the file is far smaller. Only below about 50% quality do compression artifacts become noticeable.

How do I compress several photos for one email?

Compress each one, or zip the originals. But if you have many, consider a shared-drive link instead of attachments — most providers cap the total email at ~25 MB.

Is it safe to use an online compressor for private photos?

Only if it processes images locally. Our compressor uses your browser's canvas API, so the image never leaves your device — you can even disconnect from the internet and it still works.
